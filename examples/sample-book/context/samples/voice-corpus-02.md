# Voice Corpus 02: Asking for Feedback That Actually Helps

Most requests for feedback are too broad to answer honestly. "What did you think?" invites a polite shrug, not a useful response. Ask a narrower question instead. Give the other person permission to be specific.

Start with the moment, not the whole piece. Instead of asking whether your proposal was good, ask whether your second section made its case clearly enough to act on. A narrow question signals that you already have a view of your own weak points. It gives your reader a concrete place to stand, and you will get sharper answers this way, almost every time you ask it well.

Pick your reader before you pick your question. A colleague who admires your work in general terms is a fine cheerleader, but a weak editor. Look for someone who has done your specific task recently enough to remember what the difficult parts were. Their standards will be higher than yours right now, and higher standards from someone who has actually done the work tend to be worth keeping close.

Ask before your work is finished, not after. Feedback on a draft can still change your draft. Feedback on a finished piece mostly just confirms what you already suspected, good or bad, and confirmation is not improvement. Wait until the last responsible moment, and you have already spent the flexibility that made your feedback worth asking for.

Here is a habit worth building. Write down your specific question before you send the request. "What do you think" becomes "does my opening earn the reader's attention in the initial three sentences." That single change usually improves the quality of what comes back, more than politeness or persistence ever could on their own.

Tell your reader what kind of feedback you actually want. Say whether you are looking for line-level observations or a structural read, because those are different jobs, and asking for both at once usually gets you neither done well. If you only have twenty minutes of someone's attention, spend it on the question that matters most to you this week, not the one that is easiest to answer.

## Handling the answer

Do not defend your work in the moment. You wanted an honest read, and defending your choices in real time teaches your reader to soften the next answer. Take the note. Ask one clarifying question if you genuinely need it. Save your case for afterward, once you have had time to decide whether the feedback actually changes anything you plan to do.

Separate the feedback you disagree with from the feedback you simply do not want to hear. These feel identical in the moment, and they are not the same thing at all. One is a genuine difference in judgment. The other is discomfort wearing the costume of disagreement. Give yourself a full day before you sort the two apart, since same-day sorting almost always favors your comfort over your accuracy.

Not every observation deserves equal weight from you, and treating it that way is its own mistake. Weigh the source, the specificity, and whether the same point shows up more than once, from more than one reader. A single strong objection from someone who knows your terrain carries more than three vague compliments from people who do not.

Close the loop when you can. Tell your reader what you changed because of their note, even briefly. This costs you almost nothing, and it does something feedback requests rarely account for. It teaches your sharpest readers that their time with your work was not wasted, which is exactly why they keep offering you their sharpest thinking instead of their kindest.

Not everything you make needs feedback. Save your narrow, specific request for the work that actually matters to you this quarter, and let the rest go out into the world without a formal review. Not every piece you write earns the cost of someone else's careful attention, and pretending otherwise wears out goodwill you will need afterward, when the stakes are higher for you and an honest eye really is required.

A last note on timing, since it matters as much as the question itself. Ask early enough that a real answer is still possible for you to use. Ask specifically enough that the answer, once it arrives, is one you can actually act on. Both halves matter to the outcome. Neither one works alone, and skipping either turns a useful habit into an empty ritual that only looks like diligence to whoever is watching you do it.

## A short script, if you need one

If you don't know how to open the request, here's a script. Say what you made. Say what worried you about it. Ask your one narrow question. Stop talking.

Do not apologize for asking. You're not wasting anyone's time; you are respecting it, by being specific instead of vague. A vague request costs your reader more effort than a sharp one, not less.

If you are nervous about the answer, say so out loud before you ask. "I'd rather hear this now than find out afterward" is a fine thing to tell someone. It tells your reader you can handle a real answer, and that's often exactly what gets you one.

Do not ask five people the same broad question and average their answers. You will end up with mush. Ask one or two people the narrow question instead, and you will get something you can actually use by Friday.
