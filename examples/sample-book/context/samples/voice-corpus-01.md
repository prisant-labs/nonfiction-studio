# Voice Corpus 01: The Colleague Who Answered My Question Twice

A junior product manager asked me the same question in two different meetings, three weeks apart. The first time, the answer sounded like advice. The second time, it sounded like a habit she was trying to build. That difference stuck with me for years.

The question was simple. How do you know which feedback to act on, and which to let pass? Most people answer this with a rule about seniority, as if a title settled the matter. It doesn't. The people whose feedback actually moves work forward are not always the loudest in the room. They are rarely the ones who speak first.

Watch a working team for a few weeks and a pattern appears. One person asks a clarifying question before every objection. Another restates the problem before proposing a fix. A third says almost nothing during the meeting, then sends a short observation afterward that reframes the whole discussion. None of these people are performing expertise. They are practicing a specific kind of attention, and attention, unlike charisma, can be learned.

Early in my own career, I mistook volume for value. If someone spoke with confidence, I assumed they had done the reading. Confidence and preparation often travel together, but they are not the same thing. A room full of confident people can still be wrong, together, at length. The correction came slowly. I started tracking whose comments changed a decision, not whose comments got the most nods.

Three names showed up on that list more than any others. None held the most senior title in the group. What they shared was a habit: asking one more question before agreeing with the room. It slowed meetings down by a minute or two. It also caught two mistakes that would have cost a full quarter to unwind, quietly, without anyone noticing until much afterward.

Here is the part that took me longer to learn. This habit is not a fixed trait you either have or lack. It is closer to a posture, one you practice the way a runner practices pacing before a race matters. Notice when you are agreeing out of momentum, not conviction. Notice how often you speak in the first five minutes of a meeting, and whether that speed serves the group, or just your own discomfort with silence.

A mentor once said something I didn't understand at the time. The fastest way to lose a room's trust is to be right too quickly, too often, in a way that leaves no space for anyone else's thinking. Being right isn't the same as being useful. A team that only hears from its fastest talker eventually stops offering its slower, better ideas at all.

None of this means silence is a strategy on its own. Plenty of people say nothing, not because they are weighing their words, but because they have nothing to add. The habit worth building is not quiet for its own sake. It is a specific kind of readiness. Stay close enough to the problem that when you finally speak, the room can tell the difference between filler and signal.

The habit shows up in any new meeting, with people not yet familiar. Who asks the second question, not the first? Who restates the problem before reaching for a fix? Those small moves, more than any title on the invite, signal who is worth the closest attention for the rest of the hour.

## What changed my own practice

I started keeping a short list after important meetings: what did I hear that I would have missed, had I spoken first? Some weeks the list stayed empty. Most weeks it had at least one entry, and that entry usually pointed to something worth a follow-up, not during the meeting, but soon after.

The product manager who asked her question twice eventually stopped asking it. Not because she got a sharper rule from me. She built the habit the rule had only pointed toward, and once the habit took hold, the question mostly answered itself. That is usually how this works, in my own experience. Advice rarely survives contact with practice unless the practice gets enough room, and enough time, to take over.

If you manage people, watch for this pattern before assuming you already know your best listeners. They are not always your best talkers. The gap between the two groups is where a surprising amount of a team's genuine judgment quietly lives, waiting for someone patient enough to notice it.

A year after that second meeting, the same product manager turned up at a conference. She said she now asks a version of her old question of her own team, once a quarter, and writes down who answers it without being asked twice. That's the tell: she'd turned an old habit into her own, and made it sharper along the way. A genuine practice usually travels like that, not as advice repeated, but as a habit rebuilt, once, by someone willing to sit with the discomfort long enough to make it theirs.

## A smaller version of the same test

Not every team gets a full quarter to build this habit properly. Most get a single meeting, and a single chance to show whether the pattern holds under real pressure. Watch what happens the moment a decision gets genuinely difficult. Do the same three names ask the slower question, or does everyone default to whoever speaks loudest once the stakes rise?

I ran a version of this test once with a much smaller team, four people, over a single difficult quarter. The pattern held. The two people who asked the slower questions in easy weeks kept asking them in difficult weeks too, when it would have been simpler to just agree with the room and move on. Their questions cost the team maybe an extra ten minutes across those months. They also caught a pricing mistake that would have taken a full release cycle to unwind.

None of this scales into a formula you can apply blindly. Teams differ, stakes differ, and a habit built under one set of pressures does not always survive a different one intact. What travels well is the underlying test: watch who slows a room down on purpose, not by accident, and notice whether the room is better for it afterward. Usually it is. Occasionally it is not, and that difference is worth studying on its own.

## What organizational research suggests

Researchers who study workplace communication have spent considerable attention on this exact distinction, separating confident participation from genuinely useful contribution. Their consistent finding across dozens of workplace observations is unglamorous: professionals whose input measurably improves group decisions are rarely identifiable through vocal presence alone. Instead, the pattern points toward a narrower behavioral signature, something closer to deliberate restraint paired with well-timed intervention.

Organizational psychologists describe this signature using several overlapping concepts: psychological safety, distributed leadership, and information elaboration. Information elaboration in particular deserves attention here, since it names the specific mechanism this whole practice depends on. A group elaborates information well when members surface disagreements and unshared considerations before converging on a decision, rather than defaulting immediately toward apparent consensus.

Professionals who practice this deliberately, across repeated interactions, tend to accumulate a reputation disproportionate to their formal seniority. Colleagues learn, gradually and without explicit instruction, whose objections warrant genuine reconsideration and whose agreement can be assumed without much scrutiny. This reputation compounds slowly and erodes quickly, which explains why so few professionals manage to build and sustain it across an entire organization.

A colleague once asked whether this made meetings slower overall. It does, slightly, and that cost is worth naming honestly rather than waved away. What it buys in return, most quarters, is a smaller number of expensive mistakes that never had the chance to compound into something worse.
