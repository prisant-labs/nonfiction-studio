# Voice Corpus 01: The Question I Kept Asking Wrong

I used to think a good question was a clever one. I've since learned it's usually the opposite. The clever question shows off what I already know. The good question admits what I don't, and that's a much harder sentence to say out loud than it looks.

Here's the moment that taught me. I was maybe two years into my first real job, sitting across from a woman named Terri who ran the department I wanted to move into. I'd prepared a question I thought was sharp: I asked her how she'd balanced three competing priorities on a project I'd read about in a memo. She looked at me for a second and said, "I don't think that's actually what you want to know." She was right. I didn't care about her prioritization framework. I wanted to know if I was allowed to fail in front of her and still be trusted afterward. I just hadn't found the nerve to ask that.

So I asked it. Badly, haltingly, with my ears turning red. And she answered it directly, which surprised me more than anything else that year. She told me about a launch she'd botched two levels up from where I was standing, and how her own boss hadn't pulled her off the project, just asked her what she'd do differently. That's the whole story. It's not dramatic. But I've carried it for fifteen years because it's the first time someone showed me that the real question is rarely the one I'd rehearsed.

I've gotten better at this, though I wouldn't say I've mastered it. I still catch myself building a polished question when what I actually need is a messy one. I'll draft an email three times, sanding off every rough edge, before I realize I'm not asking anymore, I'm performing. When I notice that, I delete two of the three drafts and send the rough one. It's uncomfortable every single time. I don't think that discomfort ever fully goes away, and I've made a kind of peace with that.

What I didn't expect was how much this changes once you're the one being asked. I run a small team now, and I get versions of my old sharp questions all the time. Someone wants to know how I structured a particular negotiation, and I can tell within a sentence or two that they're not actually curious about the negotiation. They're curious about whether it's safe to come to me when something's gone sideways. I've started answering that question first, even when it isn't the one on the table. I'll say something like, "before I get into the details, I want you to know I've messed this up before, more than once." It changes the whole conversation. People sit back a little. They ask the real thing next.

I don't always get this right. Some weeks I'm rushed, and I answer the surface question because it's faster, and I can feel afterward that I've missed something. I try not to beat myself up about it, though I'll admit I don't always succeed at that either. What I've settled on, imperfectly, is a habit: when someone asks me something, I pause for one extra breath before I answer, and in that breath I ask myself what they're actually worried about. It's a small trick. It's not foolproof. But it's caught more than I expected it to.

I think about Terri often, more than she'd probably guess. I never told her what that conversation did for me, and I regret that a little. I've thought about writing to her. I haven't yet. Maybe I will. What I know is that I wouldn't be the kind of colleague I am now if she hadn't looked at my clever, useless question and told me, gently, that it wasn't the one I meant to ask.

I'm not saying every workplace makes room for that kind of honesty. Plenty don't, and I've worked in a few of them. But I've come to believe the room can be made smaller than it looks, one honest question at a time, even if you're not the one in charge yet. You don't need permission to ask the real thing. You just need to notice you're not asking it, and that noticing gets easier with practice. I promise you that part, at least, because it happened to me.
