# Voice Corpus 03: I Don't Have a System, and I've Stopped Apologizing for That

People ask me what my system is, and I've learned to just tell them the truth: I don't really have one. I've got habits, a few of them stubborn, and I've got a pretty high tolerance for dropping the habit for a week and picking it back up without treating the gap like a failure. That's not a system. It's closer to a truce.

I used to feel embarrassed about this. I'd read about someone's elaborate morning routine, or their color-coded notebook, and I'd think I was doing it wrong because I couldn't stick to anything that structured. I tried, more than once. I bought the notebook. I built the spreadsheet. I'd keep it up for nine days and then I'd forget for three weeks, and I'd feel like I'd failed some test I hadn't agreed to take in the first place.

What actually works for me, and I say this knowing it won't work for everyone, is much scrappier than that. I keep one running note on my phone. I don't organize it. I don't tag it. When something strikes me as worth remembering, I type it in, usually badly, usually in the middle of doing something else. I'll go weeks without opening that note again. Then I'll have a free hour, and I'll read back through it, and I'm always a little surprised by what I find there. Half of it's useless. I've made peace with that ratio.

I've noticed that the people who seem the most disciplined from the outside are often just better at hiding the mess. I had a colleague, brilliant guy, who everyone assumed had some rigorous process behind his best ideas. I asked him once, and he laughed and said he mostly just talked to himself in the car. That was it. That was the whole system. I don't think he was being falsely modest. I think we'd both built our best thinking out of something closer to chaos than either of us wanted to admit in a meeting.

I'm not against structure. I want to be clear about that, because I don't want this to read as an argument against trying. I've just stopped believing that the structure has to look impressive to work. My running note isn't impressive. It's not even organized. But I go back to it more than I ever went back to that color-coded notebook, because it doesn't ask anything of me. It doesn't make me feel like I'm behind before I've even opened it.

The habit I've actually kept, the one that's stuck for years now, is smaller than any of that. Once a week, usually on a day I don't plan for it, I ask myself one question: what's one thing I understood better this week than I did last week. I don't write an essay about it. I don't even always write it down. I just ask, and I sit with the answer for a minute. Some weeks the answer is thin. I'll admit that. Some weeks I can't come up with much of anything, and I used to feel bad about those weeks. I don't anymore. I've decided a thin week isn't a failed week, just an honest one.

I think the reason this small habit outlasted every elaborate one I tried is that it doesn't require me to be a different person than I actually am. I'm not a person who maintains a spreadsheet. I've accepted that about myself, mostly without resentment. I'm a person who forgets things, remembers them at strange times, and occasionally asks himself a decent question in the shower. I've stopped trying to build a system around the person I wished I were, and I've started building small, forgiving habits around the person I actually am. It's a lower bar. I've found I clear it far more often than I ever cleared the higher one.

If you're waiting to feel disciplined before you start, I don't think that feeling is coming, not in the form you're picturing. I wasn't waiting for it either, not anymore. I just started asking myself one honest question a week, and I let the rest stay messy. It's still messy. I've made my peace with that, and most weeks, that's more than enough.

I'll tell you the part I still find embarrassing, though. I used to hide the messiness whenever someone new joined my team. I'd tidy the running note before I let anyone see my screen. I'd talk about "my process" like it was a real thing, capital P, when really it was just a phone note and a weekly question. I don't do that anymore. I've started showing people the actual note, typos and all, because I think the tidied-up version taught them the wrong lesson. It taught them that competence looks orderly. I don't believe that's true, not for me and not for most of the people I've admired.

I've also noticed I judge other people's mess less than I used to. When a colleague tells me they don't have a real system either, I don't hear it as a confession anymore. I hear it as something closer to relief, theirs and mine both. I'll usually say so. I'll say, "I don't either, and I've stopped pretending." It's a small thing to say out loud, but I've watched it change a conversation more than once. People relax. They stop performing the notebook they don't actually keep.

I don't know if I'll ever build the elaborate system I once thought I was supposed to have. I've stopped expecting to. What I've got instead is smaller, scrappier, and mine, and I've decided that's not a consolation prize. It's just what worked, once I stopped auditioning for a version of discipline I was never going to feel.
